# Does publishing a working strategy destroy it?

```
WRITTEN: 2026-11-06, BEFORE the informed run, deliberately.
A publication policy decided after seeing a result is not a policy.
```

## The effect is real and measured

Publication decay is documented, not folklore. McLean & Pontiff (2016),
*Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?*, Journal of
Finance, tracked 97 anomalies and found roughly a 58% fall in returns after
publication — of which about 32 points is in-sample bias unwinding and
about 26 points is attributable to publication itself, i.e. people reading
it and trading it.

So the concern is legitimate. **It does not apply to ARM F part A, for two
specific reasons.**

## 1. It is already published

RSI2 below 20 above the 200-day average, held a few days, is one of the
best-known retail mean-reversion setups in existence — commonly associated
with Larry Connors' short-term trading work, and reproduced in countless
blog posts, books and TradingView scripts for roughly two decades.

**We did not discover it. We verified it.** The publication-decay channel
has already been fully exploited by everyone else; there is nothing left
for us to leak. If anything, the fact that it is crowded is a live
hypothesis for why the first ten weeks of unseen data came in at 4% of the
backtested edge.

## 2. Capacity is not the binding constraint

Decay through crowding bites where capacity is small — microcaps, illiquid
instruments, narrow windows. ARM F part A trades spx500usd, nas100usd and
de30eur: three of the most liquid instruments in the world, on a $10,000
account, at one trade every eleven days.

If every viewer of the channel traded it at retail size, the effect on
index CFD pricing would be unmeasurable. The capacity argument that
protects a hedge fund's edge simply does not describe this situation.

## The risk that IS real, and it is not the one asked about

Publishing something that **looks** viable, that viewers trade, that then
fails forward.

That is a reputational and ethical exposure rather than an economic one,
and it is larger than the crowding risk. The mitigation is already in the
material and must stay in it:

- **5 of 7, not endorsed.** It failed robustness, and the failure is
  specific and narrow.
- **The forward test is pending**, registered, and twelve months long.
- **Paper only** until it clears, and the honest bar is ~150 forward trades
  — roughly four years, not one.
- **The reason it looks best is the reason to distrust it**: it is the most
  heavily searched object in the study.

A viewer who watches the episode and comes away thinking "here is a system
I should trade" has been failed by the edit, not by the data.

## Withholding would be the thing we criticise

Preregistration section 6 commits to publishing the gate results whichever
way they come out. Publishing five failures and holding back the one that
survived is precisely the selective disclosure this project exists to
expose — the genre's move, inverted. It would also be noticed: the prompts,
the specifications and the method are all published, so anyone could
re-run it and find the result we left out.

## The policy, fixed now

```
1. Everything is published: all six arms, all gate results, the one that
   survived included.
2. Nothing is presented as a recommendation. The verdict language is
   "cleared N of 7", never "it works".
3. If a future arm -- the informed run especially -- produces something
   that is BOTH novel and capacity-constrained, this policy is revisited
   BEFORE that result is seen, not after. Novelty and capacity are the two
   conditions that would change the calculus, and neither is present here.
4. The forward test is published monthly whether it flatters the result or
   not.
```

Point 3 is the one that matters for what comes next. The informed run is
designed to find something the blind arms could not. If it succeeds, the
publication question becomes a genuine one for the first time — and the
time to decide the rule is now, while the answer is still unknown.
